## Planning a visit around the 1935 earthquake story

The 1935 Hsinchu–Taichung earthquake severely damaged Longteng Bridge. The surviving remains make the disaster an essential part of understanding this railway site.

## What to know before arriving

Notice the arches, piers and remaining spans. Together, they show how the Japanese-era mountain railway was engineered to cross uneven terrain.

## What the site tells visitors

The broken bridge is not an incomplete decorative structure. Its condition reflects an earthquake, later railway engineering changes and the decision to preserve the historic remains.

## Frequently asked questions

**Why wasn't the bridge rebuilt to its original form?** Heritage preservation gives weight to historic evidence and safety; reopening a railway is not the sole measure of a successful conservation project.

**Do I need a ticket?** Viewing the outdoor monument is distinct from riding the paid Old Mountain Line Rail Bike. Check separately if booking a ride.

**Where is the correct map destination?** Navigate to [Longteng Bridge](https://maps.app.goo.gl/55jN8AsmsDdr5xGs5); rail bike guests should use their ticket's departure station.

## From railway construction to earthquake ruins

Yutengping Bridge once carried the Old Mountain Line across a valley. The 1935 earthquake caused serious structural damage. Its remaining brick arches and the replacement engineering that followed document a railway responding to natural disaster.

## Observing the historical evidence

Imagine the original direction of travel across the arches and the loads carried by the piers. Then compare the nearby iron bridge and its materials. Read on-site interpretation so the two structures are not mistaken for one project.

## Preservation and photography

Conserving a ruin does not necessarily mean reconnecting it for trains. Include the broken sections and surrounding terrain in photographs to communicate the bridge's historical meaning.
